Field Notes

Ten reference points on the mountain behind the project.

Aconcagua is a real place with specific geography, history, and cultural weight. These cards ground the experience in verified context.

Field note

The Highest Outside Asia

The highest mountain in the Americas and the highest peak on Earth outside Asia.

Aconcagua is the highest point in the Western Hemisphere — the continental reference for every altitude record outside the Himalayas and the Karakoram. Its scale defines the identity of the Andes.

Field note

Mendoza / Chile Border

Aconcagua stands in western Mendoza, right on the Chilean border.

The mountain rises in the Central Andes of western Mendoza province, Argentina, at the border with Chile. The nearest city is Mendoza, roughly 110 km to the southeast.

Field note

6,962 m

Official park reference height: 6,962 meters above sea level.

Measured surveys have returned slightly varying figures over the decades, but 6,962 m is the altitude published by park authorities and used as the working reference.

Field note

A Provincial Park

Aconcagua is protected within Parque Provincial Aconcagua.

The park is managed by the province of Mendoza. The Horcones entrance sits at roughly 2,800 m, about 185 km west of the city by road.

Field note

Main Season: Nov–Apr

The official summer season runs from November 1 to April 30.

Permits and services operate during the austral summer. Most summit attempts concentrate in December and January, when weather windows are widest.

Field note

Volcanic Origin

Aconcagua is of volcanic origin, but it is not an active volcano.

The massif formed through volcanic activity millions of years ago and was later uplifted by tectonic compression. Today it is classified as an extinct volcano — imposing, but geologically quiet.

Field note

Sentinel of Stone

The name Aconcagua may derive from Quechua and is often linked to the idea of a "stone sentinel."

The etymology is debated. One widely cited interpretation links the name to Quechua roots meaning "stone sentinel." Other scholars propose Mapudungun or Aymara origins. No single derivation is settled.

Field note

First Ascent / 1897

The first successful ascent was made in 1897 by Matthias Zurbriggen.

Swiss mountain guide Matthias Zurbriggen reached the summit on January 14, 1897, during an expedition led by Edward FitzGerald. The party approached via the Normal Route from the northwest.

Field note

An Inca Ceremonial Mountain

Aconcagua also forms part of a wider Andean archaeological and sacred landscape.

The mountain belongs to the broader Andean sacred geography connected to the Qhapaq Ñan, the Inca road system recognized by UNESCO. High-altitude sites across the Andes served as ceremonial platforms. Aconcagua's cultural significance predates European contact by centuries.

Field note

The Aconcagua Child

In 1985, climbers found the frozen remains of a 7-year-old Inca child on the Aconcagua massif.

The remains were discovered at approximately 5,300 m on the southwest ridge. Archaeological study identified them as those of a boy of around seven years, sacrificed over 500 years ago in what researchers interpret as a capacocha offering — an Inca state ritual of profound religious significance.
